How Seeing Gets Built

Look at this courtyard. It feels as if your eyes simply take it in, like a camera. They don't.

In the next few minutes you'll watch your brain build this picture from nothing: first dots, then signals, then edges, then things, then guesses. The studies switch on in roughly the order scientists made them, with a few textbook facts in between.

Scroll or swipe to begin. Sound is off; the button at the top turns it on.

1851Weber

Can a feeling be measured?

Before anyone could study seeing, someone had to show that a sensation can be measured at all.

Ernst Weber pressed two pin points on people's skin. Close together, they felt like one point. He measured the exact gap where "two" turns into "one".

This was about touch, not sight. But it started a big idea: what you experience can be put into numbers.

1867Helmholtz

Your brain works it out

Hermann von Helmholtz argued that seeing is not a snapshot. Your eye only sends signals. Your brain works out what's out there, quickly and without you noticing.

He called this unconscious inference. Inference means reaching an answer from clues. Remember the word clues: it runs through this whole film.

1912Wertheimer

Movement that isn't there

Watch the two lamps in the middle arch. Each one just switches on and off, one after the other. Nothing travels between them.

Yet you probably see one light hopping back and forth. Max Wertheimer showed people exactly this kind of display. The movement exists only in the brain.

It's early proof that what you see is something your brain makes.

Textbook factInside the eye

The picture turns into dots

Now follow the light inside. It passes through the lens and lands on the retina, a thin sheet of cells lining the back of your eye.

Here the picture stops being a picture. It becomes dots. Each dot is a light-catching cell called a photoreceptor. You have around 90 to 120 million rods, which work in dim light, and about 4.5 to 6 million cones, which see colour. Textbooks give different counts.

Cones are packed tightly in the very centre, the fovea, so only the middle of what you look at is sharp. Try reading a word two lines away without moving your eyes.

Sources
  • Purves et al., Neuroscience (2nd ed.): 91 million rods, about 4.5 million cones, and the fovea. NCBI Bookshelf
  • American Academy of Ophthalmology: about 120 million rods, 6 million cones. aao.org
  • The dot pattern is an illustration: denser in the centre, like the fovea.
1938Hartline

Your eye talks in bursts

Those cells don't send a picture to the brain. They send spikes: tiny electrical blips, like clicks.

H. Keffer Hartline listened to a single nerve fibre from an animal's eye. It fired a burst when a light switched on, or off, or both, depending on the fibre.

Watch the dots: now they only flash when something changes, like the bird crossing. Vision travels as coded messages, not as a moving picture.

Sources
  • Hartline, H. K. (1938). American Journal of Physiology 121, 400. doi
  • Pixel Hound record (Seeing stop 4). Flashing only on change is our illustration of on/off firing.
Textbook factThe cable and the hole

A blind spot you never notice

The spikes leave each eye along the optic nerve, a cable of about 1.2 million fibres. That's far fewer than the light-catching cells, so the retina squeezes the message down first.

Where the cable leaves the eye there are no light-catching cells at all. That makes a blind spot in each eye. You never see the hole, because your brain fills it in. Watch it close.

Most signals then pass through a relay station in the middle of the brain, the thalamus, and on to the back of your head: the primary visual cortex, or V1.

Sources

  • Purves et al., the path through the thalamus to V1. NCBI Bookshelf
  • The size and place of the hole here are exaggerated so you can see it.
1959Hubel & Wiesel

Cells that love one angle

This is where the picture starts being rebuilt. David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel recorded single brain cells in cats while showing them lines of light.

A cell would fire for a line at one exact tilt. Turn the line and the cell went quiet. Watch the cell in the corner: it only fires when the bar reaches its favourite angle. Turn sound on to hear it.

Now the courtyard is drawn the way such cells see it: short lines, each coloured by its tilt.

Sources

1962Hubel & Wiesel

Two eyes, one view

Your two eyes see from slightly different spots. Close one eye, then the other, and things jump.

Three years later Hubel and Wiesel mapped how these cells are arranged. They showed where signals from the two eyes come together. Watch the two views slide into one.

1968Hubel & Wiesel

A brain sorted in columns

In monkeys they found that the seeing part of the brain is organised in columns. Picture its surface as a sheet of tiny towers standing side by side.

Each tower is sorted by line angle and by which eye is in charge. The swirl of colours is a sketch of that kind of map. Each colour stands for an angle, matching the lines in the courtyard.

The swirl is our illustration, not their data.

1980Treisman & Gelade

Attention is the glue

Colour and shape are handled separately. Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade argued that attention, what you focus on, is what glues them into one object.

Here the colours have drifted off their shapes. Only inside the spotlight do they snap back. On a computer, move your mouse to steer it.

That's why finding a red circle among red squares and blue circles means checking items one at a time.

Sources

1997Kanwisher, McDermott & Chun

A patch just for faces

Further along, the brain sorts what it sees into kinds of things.

Brain scans found a small patch that lights up more for faces than for objects, hands or houses. It's called the fusiform face area.

Did your eyes go to the person in the arch before anything else?

1999Rao & Ballard

Predict, then check

Here's the twist. Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard proposed that higher parts of the brain predict what lower parts should be seeing. Mostly, only the mistakes get passed on.

It's like a teacher who only marks what's wrong. Watch: the still courtyard fades, because it was predicted. Only the bird, the surprise, stays bright.

This was a computer model, a proposal, not an experiment on people.

Sources
  • Rao, R. P. N. & Ballard, D. H. (1999). Predictive coding in the visual cortex. Nature Neuroscience. doi
  • Pixel Hound record (Seeing stop 10).
2015Muckli et al.

Seeing what's hidden

Is there evidence for guessing? Lars Muckli's team used powerful brain scanners while part of a scene was hidden from people.

Early seeing areas still carried information about the hidden part, worked out from the surroundings, even where the eye sent nothing.

The square hides a corner of the courtyard. Your brain still "knows" roughly what's behind it.

Sources
  • Muckli, L. et al. (2015). Contextual feedback to superficial layers of V1. Current Biology. PMC
  • Pixel Hound record (Seeing stop 11).
2016Animal study

When the expected thing is missing

Mice ran along a virtual corridor, a video-game hallway. Their seeing cells learned what should appear at each spot.

When an expected sight was missing, those cells fired hard. Here the fountain vanishes, and the place where it should be lights up with surprise.

Sources
  • Experience-dependent spatial expectations in mouse visual cortex (2016). Nature Neuroscience. doi

2015–2016Your body in the picture

Is that hand yours?

Seeing isn't only about the world. It's also about you.

Hide your real hand. Stroke a rubber hand at the same moment as your hidden one. After a while, the rubber hand starts to feel like yours. Samad, Chung and Shams showed that this feeling followed a simple rule that combines what you see, feel and sense. "My hand" is a best guess.

Other researchers turned fake-hand and whole-body illusions into tools for science. In 2016 Manos Tsakiris pulled it together: your sense of self is built from signals from outside and inside the body, as a kind of prediction.

2016–2024Signals from inside

Your heartbeat is in the picture too

Your brain also tracks signals from inside you: heartbeat, breathing, gut. Sensing them is called interoception.

Some people sense their heartbeat well but not their breath, or the other way round (Garfinkel's team, 2016). How well you do it, how well you think you do, and how aware you are can all differ.

A 2019 study found that where you put your attention changes how your brain responds to your own heartbeat. That's recent and still being tested. A 2024 paper sets out a plan: to explain feelings, first map how signals travel from the heart, gut and lungs to the brain. It's a plan, not a result yet.

2020–2022The whole stack

Signals up, guesses down

Put it all together and you get a stack: dots, then edges, then colour and things. Signals climb up. Guesses flow down.

Christoph Teufel and Paul Fletcher argued in 2020 that predictions don't only come down from the "smart" top. They also form low down, near the senses.

And the biggest question is still open: how does any of this become the experience of seeing? A 2022 review compared four leading theories of consciousness and asked which experiments could finally tell them apart.

Sources
  • Teufel, C. & Fletcher, P. C. (2020). Nature Reviews Neuroscience. doi
  • Theories of consciousness (2022). Nature Reviews Neuroscience. doi

The picture, again

Now look at the courtyard

Same picture. But now you know what it is. Not light in a camera, but a best guess, built from dots, spikes, edges and memories, and checked against the light again and again for as long as you're awake.

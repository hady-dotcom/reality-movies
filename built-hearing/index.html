1863Helmholtz

Notes you can take apart

Hermann von Helmholtz studied pitch, harmony, and why some notes sound good together.

His big move was to treat hearing like any other part of the body: something you can pull apart and study. One musical note turns out to be a stack of simpler tones called harmonics. Press play: you'll hear the note, then each layer on its own, then the note again.

Textbook factThe outer and middle ear

From air to bone

Sound is a wobble in the air: air pressure going up and down very fast. How fast it wobbles each second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). Humans hear from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Adults usually lose the very top first.

The wobble travels down your ear canal and shakes the eardrum, a thin skin stretched across it. Three tiny bones pass the shaking on to the cochlea, which is filled with fluid. Pushing air against fluid would bounce almost all the sound back. So the eardrum and the bones act like a funnel and a lever, and they boost the pressure almost 200 times.

Textbook factThe cochlea

A piano keyboard inside your head

The cochlea is a coiled tube about the shape of a snail shell. Here it's drawn unrolled. Along it runs a strip called the basilar membrane. It's narrow and stiff at the entrance (the base) and wide and floppy at the far end (the tip).

So high notes shake the base hardest, and low notes shake the tip. Each spot is tuned to one pitch, like the keys on a piano. Scientists call this tonotopy, meaning "a map of tones".

Sitting on the strip are hair cells, in one row of inner cells and three rows of outer cells. When their tiny hairs bend, they make electrical signals. About 95% of the nerve fibres going to the brain come from the inner row.

Sources
  • Purves et al., The Inner Ear (high notes at the base, low at the tip). NCBI Bookshelf
  • Purves et al., Two Kinds of Hair Cells (rows, 95%). NCBI Bookshelf
  • The strip and its pitch labels are an illustration, not measured positions.
Textbook factThe road to the brain

Five stops up

The signals leave along the auditory nerve (auditory just means "to do with hearing"). They climb through a chain of stations. Each one keeps the piano-keyboard order.

First the brainstem, at the base of the brain. Parts of it compare your two ears to work out where a sound is coming from. Next the midbrain, then the thalamus, a relay that every hearing signal must pass through to reach the top. Last, the hearing area of the cortex, the brain's wrinkly outer layer. Keep this ladder in mind: it comes back later.

Sources
  • Purves et al., How Information from the Cochlea Reaches the Brainstem. NCBI Bookshelf
  • Purves et al., The Auditory Thalamus (the relay every signal passes through). NCBI Bookshelf
  • The ladder is simplified, and the real pathway has more stations.

The cocktail party problem

1957Speech sounds

Sounds snap into boxes

A speech sound like "ba" can be slid smoothly, step by step, into "da". But we don't hear the slide. We hear one sound or the other, with hardly anything in between.

Scientists call this categorical perception: your brain sorts sounds into boxes, or categories.

1964Treisman

The other ear is still listening

Anne Treisman found that when you follow a voice in one ear, the other ear gets turned down, not off. Meaning can still sneak through from the ear you're ignoring.

So your brain is quietly checking the sounds you think you're ignoring.

1990 · 2005Bregman, then monkeys

Your brain splits the mix

Albert Bregman argued that your ears don't receive one soundtrack. Your brain groups sound into streams: who is talking, which instrument is playing.

In 2005, researchers played the same mix of beeps for a while and recorded from the hearing areas of monkeys. One stream of sound turned into two over time.

Press play and listen: a galloping "da-di-da" speeds up. For many people it splits into two separate lines of beeps, one high and one low.

2016Pattern spotting

A pattern-spotter in your head

Listeners heard fast beeps. Sometimes the beeps were random, and sometimes a hidden pattern started repeating. People spotted the pattern almost as well as a perfect calculator would.

2016 · 2017Dürschmid; Parras

Surprise is flagged on the way up

When a sound breaks a pattern, the hearing area and the front of the brain both raise a flag (Dürschmid and colleagues, 2016). So surprise is checked at more than one level.

In 2017, Parras and colleagues found that "that wasn't expected" signals got stronger step by step, from the midbrain, through the thalamus, up to the hearing area. Surprise isn't only handled at the top.

2017The beat of speech

Speech pulses about 5 times a second

Researchers measured 25 hours of speech in nine languages and 39 hours of music. Speech rises and falls about 5 times a second, roughly one syllable at a time. Music is closer to 2.

2016–2020Sound meets sight

The ventriloquist trick

What you see pulls where you think a sound is coming from. That's why a puppet seems to talk (Tong, Li, Bruns and Röder, 2020). Learned sight-and-sound pairs change how strong that pull is.

A 2016 review asked when a sound and a sight join into one thing, and how scientists can prove it. A 2017 review of brain-scan studies found that patterns of sound change activity in the very first seeing area. Hearing gives your eyes context.

2025Animal study

Did that match my step?

In mice, the hearing part of the brain flags a sound that doesn't match their movement. Surprises from the eyes feed into the same signal.

Your brain expects your own footsteps, and it notices when one is off.

The sound, again

Now listen to the courtyard

The fountain, the voice, your own footsteps. None of it reaches you as a finished soundtrack. Your ears break each sound into pitches. Your brain sorts them into streams, checks them against what you see, and keeps guessing what comes next.

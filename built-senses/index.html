All Your Senses

At school you probably learned that you have five senses: sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. That list goes back about 2,300 years, to the Greek thinker Aristotle.

Scientists now think it's far too short. Walk through this courtyard at golden hour, the warm last hour of sunlight, and watch each sense switch on where it naturally happens. Some will surprise you, because they work all day without you noticing.

A sense here means a kind of special cell (a receptor) that reacts to one kind of thing, such as light, heat or stretch, and sends a signal to your brain.

Scroll or swipe to begin. Sound is off; the button at the top turns it on. The counter at the top keeps score.

Sense 1 · Through the arch

Sight: four kinds of light-catcher

First, the light pouring through the arches. At the back of each eye is the retina, a thin sheet of light-catching cells.

Rods work in dim light but don't see colour. Cones see colour, and come in three types, tuned to short (bluish), medium (greenish) and long (reddish) light. Every colour you see is your brain comparing those three.

rods (dim light)short conesmedium coneslong cones

So is sight one sense, or four? That question is the whole puzzle of this film. Keep it in mind.

Sense 2 · Sun on your skin

Warm and cold are separate senses

Step into the sunbeam. The warmth on your arm is a different sense from touch. It's called thermoreception: "thermo" means heat.

Your skin has nerve endings for warming and others for cooling. In the 1990s and 2000s, David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian found the molecules that do it: a heat sensor called TRPV1 (the same one chilli peppers set off) and a cold sensor called TRPM8 (the one menthol, the cool in mint, sets off). They won the 2021 Nobel Prize for it.

warmcold

That's why chilli feels "hot" and mint feels "cool" even at room temperature: they trick the temperature sensors.

Sources

Sense 3 · The jasmine

Smell: about 400 locks, endless keys

Jasmine flowers release tiny floating molecules. You breathe them in, and they reach a small patch of nerve cells high inside your nose. Each of those cells carries one type of odour receptor.

Humans have roughly 400 working receptor types. Think of them as locks. One smell molecule usually fits several locks, and your brain reads the combination as "jasmine".

How many smells can you tell apart? A 2014 study said over a trillion. Other scientists showed the maths behind it was fragile, so nobody knows the real number yet.

Contested: the "trillion smells" number

Sense 4 · Bare feet on cool tiles

Touch unfolds into a family

Walk barefoot across the tiles. "Touch" feels like one thing, but your skin has several kinds of touch nerve, each tuned to a different job:

light touch & fluttersteady pressurevibrationskin stretchgentle stroking

Pressure endings tell you how hard your foot presses. Vibration endings pick up the faint buzz of the fountain pump through the floor. Stretch endings notice your skin pulling as your toes bend. A molecule called PIEZO2, a tiny pressure-sensitive gate in the nerve, is needed for much of this.

The last one is a surprise. Slow, gentle stroking (like a hand on your arm) has its own slow nerves, called C-tactile fibres. They seem to signal that touch feels pleasant.

The coolness of the tiles? That's your cold sense from the last stop, working alongside touch.

Sense 5 · Up the steps

Balance: a spirit level in each ear

Climb the steps by the far arch. Deep inside each ear, next to the hearing part, is the vestibular system: your built-in balance sensor.

It has two kinds of part. Three looped tubes full of fluid, the semicircular canals, notice your head turning. Two small sacs, the otolith organs, contain tiny crystals ("ear stones") that slide with gravity, so they notice tilting and speeding up in a straight line.

turning (canals)tilt & straight-line motion (otoliths)

Watch the courtyard tip as you climb. Your balance sense is what tells you it's you moving, not the building.

Sense 6 · Eyes closed on the top step

Knowing where your body is

Close your eyes and touch your nose. You can, because of proprioception (say "pro-pree-oh-SEP-shun"): the sense of where your limbs are and how they're moving.

Inside your muscles are muscle spindles, tiny stretch sensors that report how long a muscle is and how fast it's changing. Where muscles join tendons, tendon organs report how hard the muscle is pulling.

limb position & movement (spindles)muscle force (tendon organs)

The PIEZO2 gate shows up again here. In 2015 mice without it in these nerves moved clumsily, and in 2016 doctors found people born without working PIEZO2 who could barely tell where their limbs were unless they looked.

Sense 7 · The fountain

Hearing: a keyboard rolled into a snail

The fountain splashes, and the air shivers with tiny pressure waves. They reach the cochlea, a snail-shaped tube in your inner ear.

Along it sit hair cells with tiny bristles on top. When a wave passes, the bristles bend and the cell fires. Cells near the wide end pick up high sounds; cells nearer the centre pick up low ones. It works a bit like a piano keyboard, rolled up.

Durie pointed out you can lose high notes but keep low ones, so you could even call hearing many senses. Most lists count it as one.

Sense 8 · A pomegranate

Five tastes, maybe six

Bite a pomegranate seed. Your taste buds, about 10,000 at birth, sit on your tongue, the roof of your mouth and your throat. Their cells respond to at least five basic tastes:

sweetsoursaltybitterumamifat? (debated)

Umami means "savoury". Kikunae Ikeda named it in 1909 after studying a seaweed broth; it's the taste of glutamate, found in broth and cheese. The old "tongue map", with sweet on the tip, is wrong: each kind of taste cell is spread all over.

Some scientists argue fat has its own taste too, nicknamed oleogustus. Not everyone agrees yet.

And most of a pomegranate's flavour is actually smell, drifting up the back of your throat. That's why food tastes flat when you have a cold.

Contested: fat as a sixth taste

Sense 9 · Chilli on the table

The burn that isn't a taste

Next to the fruit is a dish of chilli. Its burn isn't one of the five tastes. It comes from what doctors call the common chemical sense: thousands of nerve endings in your eyes, nose, mouth and throat that react to irritating or cooling chemicals.

It's why onions make you cry, mint feels fresh and chilli feels hot. Some lists count it as its own sense; others fold it into touch or temperature.

chemical burn & cool

Sense 10 · A thorn and a mosquito

Pain and itch: two different alarms

Reach past the jasmine and a thorn pricks your finger. Special nerve endings called nociceptors ("noxious-detectors") react to things that could damage you. Pain experts define pain as an unpleasant experience linked to actual or possible harm, and stress that it's shaped by your brain and mood, not just the nerves.

Then a mosquito bites. Itch is not just weak pain. In 2007 scientists found a dedicated itch pathway in the spinal cords of mice, separate from pain. Pain makes you pull away; itch makes you scratch.

Senses 11+ · Sitting by the fountain

The senses that point inward

Sit down and go still. Now the courtyard fades and your body gets louder. Interoception ("inner sensing") is your brain keeping track of what's going on inside you:

heartbeatbreathinghungerthirstfull bladder

Each has its own sensors. Stretch sensors in the bladder wall tell you it's filling; a 2020 study found PIEZO2, again, helps them work, and people without it reported weaker bladder-filling feelings. Thirst comes from brain areas that track how salty and how full your blood is.

People differ in how well they can feel their own heartbeat, and some researchers think this inner sense shapes emotions. That link is still being worked out.

Sense ? · The shadows lengthen

Is time a sense?

The sun sinks and the arch shadows creep across the tiles. You can feel roughly how long you've been sitting, without a clock.

But there's no time organ: no "time receptor" like the cones for light. Instead, brain circuits seem to keep track of short intervals, from fractions of a second to minutes. That's why scientists argue about whether timing counts as a sense at all.

The big question

So how many senses?

It depends how you count. Try it: each button is a real way scientists have split the senses. Every star in the window is one sense.

12 senses

Researchers' estimates fall roughly between 21 and 33. Split finer still, for example hearing by pitch range or pain by where it's felt, and you can pass 40. Split smell by its roughly 400 receptor types and you pass 400. None of these is "wrong". They're answers to different questions.

The count itself is disputed

Back in the courtyard

All of them, all at once

Same courtyard. But now every part of it is lit by a different sense: the light, the warm stone, the jasmine, the tiles under your feet, the steps, the fountain, the fruit, the thorn, your heartbeat, the lengthening shadows.

They don't arrive one at a time. Your brain blends them into one moment, so smoothly that it feels like just "being here".
